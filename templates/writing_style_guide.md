# Writing Style Guide

Purpose: remove LLM fingerprints from any generated text — resumes, cover letters, portfolio copy, outreach messages. Jon is a professional writer; this content needs to read like his writing, not like AI output.

## Confirmed rules (from direct edits)

**No em dashes as clause connectors.** Restructure the sentence instead of reaching for "—". If two ideas need joining, use a period, a comma, or a plain conjunction.

**No negation-for-emphasis in resumes and bullets specifically.** Cut constructions like "rather than X," "not X, but Y," "not just X but Y" from compressed formats. State the positive claim directly. It doesn't need a foil to land.
- Cut: "...feel clear and trustworthy rather than opaque."
- Keep: "...feel clear and trustworthy."

Correction from site review (2026-09-06): this construction *does* appear in Jon's genuine unedited essay writing ("Not that it will replace us, but that we begin to believe in it more than we know ourselves" — from his own Substack). It's not a universal ban. In long-form/essay writing it's a real rhetorical move he uses naturally; the rule is specifically for resumes and other compressed, high-density formats where it reads as padding rather than reflection.

**Plain, direct verbs over abstracted framing.** "Uses language" beats "treats language as." "Driving a system" beats "the model behind a system." If a verb sounds like it's performing sophistication rather than saying something, replace it.

## Extrapolated rules (draft — confirm or cut)

These aren't from a direct edit yet, but they're the same family of tell. Flag anything here that's wrong or that you don't actually care about.

**Avoid stock AI vocabulary**: leverage, robust, seamless, landscape, tapestry, delve, underscore, boasts, showcase, furthermore, moreover, in today's [X] world, it's worth noting, comprehensive, holistic (unless it's the actual precise word for the thing).

**No rule-of-three padding.** AI text defaults to listing exactly three adjectives or examples for rhythm ("clear, concise, and compelling"). If a sentence has three items and could say the same thing with one or two, cut the extras.

**No insider/invented titling.** Don't coin a fancy-sounding name for something ad hoc just to make it sound like a framework, unless it's a real, named thing Jon actually built (e.g., the Diamond Design Process is real — that's fine to name).

**Jargon only when it's the precise term.** If a plain word says the same thing, use the plain word. Jargon is fine when it's load-bearing (a term a content design hiring manager expects), not when it's decoration.

**Watch sentence rhythm.** AI text tends toward uniform medium-length sentences. Real writing varies — short sentences land harder next to longer ones.

## Confirmed voice traits (from published writing, 2026-09-06 review)

Reviewed across a 2017 Medium post, a 2026 Substack essay, and two jonathanbosworth.com case studies — a span of ~9 years and three registers (journalism, personal essay, professional case study). These traits are consistent across all of them, which is what makes them real signal rather than a one-off:

**Short declarative sentence as section header, stated as a lesson.** Not "Feature 1: Data Access" but "Start from what we know." Not "Challenge Overview" but "Your assumptions are wrong." This shows up in a 2017 Medium post and a 2024 case study alike — it's a genuine, longstanding habit, not something new.

**Ends pieces (and major sections) on a short, standalone, confident sentence.** "That decision was correct." "What an incredible teacher." "The question is, who is training it and what do they want." No hedging, no summary throat-clearing — just the landing line.

**States failure or setbacks plainly, without spin.** "The form was the problem." "...and then had to shut it down." Frames the setback as instructive, not something to explain away. Don't soften this into corporate hedge language when writing in his voice.

**"Key Learnings" / "Principle learnings" as a closing bulleted recap** is a real structural habit spanning 2017–2024, not an AI-suggested format.

**Register shifts sharply between personal and professional writing.** In essays (Substack): heavy contractions, sentence fragments used on purpose, rhetorical questions, informal asides, almost no em dashes. In case studies (portfolio blog): more structured, some em dashes but used as an appositive aside ("in assumptions... — in promises...") rather than a generic joiner, numbers woven into narrative sentences rather than bullet-dumped. Match the register to the format — don't apply essay-voice looseness to a case study, or case-study structure to an essay.

**Comfortable with metaphor and aphorism, used sparingly.** "There are no dumb users, only dumb tools." Not decorative — each one is doing real work, stated once, not belabored.

## How to apply this
Every draft (resume, cover letter, portfolio copy) gets checked against this list before Jon sees it. When in doubt on the extrapolated rules, flag it rather than silently applying a guess. The confirmed voice traits are additive to the confirmed rules above, not a replacement — they describe how Jon's real writing sounds when it's working, not just what to avoid.
